Reducing a feeder with a voltage guarantee

How much of this feeder can I remove and still know every voltage to within a set threshold?

This tutorial answers that question on a six-bus feeder, small enough to check every step by hand. It builds up the idea in five steps: the linear voltage model, the error of one merge, why "it worked on my scenarios" is not enough, uncertainty sets, and finally a solvable reduced case whose answer is mapped back to every original bus. The method is robust Opti-KRON [1]; the reference page has the full mathematics.

Prerequisites: BMOPFTools, JuMP, HiGHS (any MILP solver works; use Gurobi for real feeders) and Ipopt. The complete script is examples/opti_kron/tutorial_feeder.jl, and every number below is its output.

1. The feeder and its linear voltage model

s ── b1 ── b2 ── b3
             └── b4 ── b5        100 m sections, R = 0.04 Ω, X = 0.025 Ω per phase

Loads sit at b1, b3, b4 and b5; s is an ideal 230 V source. Build the model:

net = tutorial_feeder()                 # defined in the example script
m = opti_kron_model(net; H=4)           # candidate super-nodes within 4 hops
# OptiKronModel(6 buses, 18 rows, 31 candidate pairs, H=4)

m.K maps injections (in kW, kvar) to voltage magnitudes (in p.u.): v = 1 + K u. On this feeder K is exactly LinDistFlow. The sensitivity of b2's voltage to power injected at b5 is the resistance their two paths to the source share (two sections, s–b1–b2), divided by U²:

K_P[b2,b5] = 1.5123e-03 p.u./kW;  2 × 0.04 Ω × 1 kW / (230 V)² = 1.5123e-03

A kilowatt at b5 lifts b2's voltage by 0.15%, through the impedance they share. Keep this picture in mind: K encodes shared impedance.

2. Training data

Reductions are judged on loadings, so we need some. Here, 24 hourly samples: a common daily level times independent noise per load.

U = opti_kron_load_injections(m, net, mult)  # mult[load_id][hour]
# 24 samples, 24 active coordinates, largest |v - 1| = 0.0278 p.u.

3. The error of one merge, by hand

Merge b3 into b2: b3's load moves to b2, and b2's voltage stands in for b3's.

sup = collect(1:6); sup[m.busof["b3"]] = m.busof["b2"]
opti_kron_errors(m, sup, U)     # error of every bus-phase, every sample
# b3 → b2: worst error over the samples 4.00e-03 p.u.

Moving b3's load to b2 leaves the current through s–b1–b2 unchanged, so the reduced voltage ṽb2 equals b2's original voltage (in the linear model). What is lost is the drop across the b2–b3 section, caused by b3's own load current. The error is ṽb2 − v_b3 = cᵀu, a linear function of the loading. That linearity is what makes everything below tractable.

4. Why a few scenarios are not enough

Choose a threshold Ē = 0.008 p.u. (deliberately loose, so that six buses can cluster) and ask for the fewest super-nodes. First the classical way: guarantee the bound only on the two most extreme training samples.

tot = vec(sum(U, dims=1))
scen = U[:, [argmax(tot), argmin(tot)]]
res = opti_kron_reduction(m, OptiKronHull(scen), 8e-3; optimizer=HiGHS.Optimizer)
# scenario  super-nodes ["s", "b1", "b2"]       worst sample error 1.26 Ē

Three buses suffice for those two loadings, but on the other 22 samples the error reaches 1.26 Ē. The bound holds on the scenarios and fails on other, less extreme loadings: the worst case depends on how the load is distributed, not only on how much there is.

Robust Opti-KRON instead guarantees the bound on an uncertainty set built from all samples:

# hull      super-nodes ["s", "b1", "b2", "b5"] worst sample error 0.76 Ē
# box       super-nodes ["s", "b1", "b2", "b5"] worst sample error 0.76 Ē
# ellipsoid super-nodes ["s", "b1", "b2", "b4"] worst sample error 0.50 Ē

One more super-node buys a certificate: the bound holds for every loading in the set, not just the ones you tried. The sets differ in how far they trust the data:

  • the convex hull covers every mixture of the samples;
  • the box lets each injection vary independently within its sampled range;
  • the ellipsoid follows the samples' covariance, but extends past the sampled range in directions of real variation;
  • the Wasserstein set covers distributions close to the empirical one.

Wider sets can need more super-nodes; here all three keep four.

5. A solvable reduced case

The assignment is only a decision. kron_reduce_buses turns it into a BMOPF case: devices move to their super-nodes, and the eliminated buses, which now carry no injection, are removed by exact Kron reduction.

res = opti_kron_reduction(m, OptiKronHull(U), 8e-3; optimizer=HiGHS.Optimizer,
                          keep=opti_kron_required_buses(net))
red = kron_reduce_buses(net, m, res; Ebar=8e-3)
# reduced buses: ["b1", "b2", "b5", "s"]
# equivalent lines: ["kron_eq:b2:b5"]

b3 and b4 are gone. The two sections b2–b4–b5 became one equivalent line, and the loads of b3 and b4 now sit at b2. With Ebar, the voltage limits of b2 are tightened so that b3 and b4 stay inside theirs. Solve both cases and compare:

pf_full = solve_pf(net; optimizer=ipopt)
pf_red  = solve_pf(red; optimizer=ipopt)
lifted  = lift_solution(net, red, pf_red; model=m)
# b3 (→ b2): full 225.283 V, super-node 225.886 V, lifted 225.310 V
# b4 (→ b2): full 224.689 V, super-node 225.886 V, lifted 224.734 V

The super-node voltage is the certified estimate: 0.6 V and 1.2 V off, within Ē = 1.84 V. lift_solution does better after the fact. Once the reduced case is solved, the loading u is known, so the error cᵀu can be subtracted: the lifted voltages are within 0.05 V.

6. Verify, don't trust

The certificate is for the linear model and for loadings in the set. Before relying on a reduced case:

  • check res.status == :optimal (certified and minimal) or :feasible (certified; the final master hit its time limit);
  • leave a margin on Ē for the linear-versus-AC gap; in the AC comparisons on Network reduction, Opti-KRON alone reached up to 1.04 Ē, and 1.44 Ē when a neutral reduction preceded it;
  • for important studies, run an AC power flow on the original network at the reduced solution's set-points, as in Simplifying a network.
