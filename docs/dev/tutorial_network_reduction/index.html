Reducing a four-wire feeder, step by step

Three reductions, three different answers to "what may I forget?"

You have a detailed four-wire LV model and a study that would run faster on a smaller one. This tutorial takes one feeder through the three reductions in BMOPFTools, in the order you would use them in practice, and at each step asks the same two questions: what did it remove, and what did that cost? By the end you will know when each tool is exact, when it is not, and how to check.

Network reduction compares the tools on real feeders; this page shows the same trade-offs on one feeder you can rerun.

Prerequisites: Reducing a feeder with a voltage guarantee, which introduces super-nodes, the hop limit H, uncertainty sets and the lift on a six-bus feeder. Packages: BMOPFTools, JuMP, HiGHS and Ipopt. The complete script is examples/opti_kron/reduction_workflow.jl (about 30 s); the code blocks below are excerpts of it and use names defined there (train, test, with_loads, ipopt), and every number is its output, shown as comments. The script generates the feeder, so no external data are needed.

1. The feeder

mv ──Dyn── lv0 ── p1 ── p2 ── … ── p12        trunk, 40 m spans
                        │           │
                        q1 … q4     r1 … r4   laterals at p4 and p8, 30 m spans

An 11 kV / 415 V transformer feeds a four-core cable trunk with two laterals. Thirty single-phase customers sit on rotating phases, two per loaded pole; five poles carry no customer. The neutral is solidly earthed at the transformer and, as is common in practice, through finite MEN electrodes (10 Ω) at every third pole.

net = synthetic_lv_feeder()
# feeder: 22 buses, 20 lines, 30 customers, 4 MEN electrodes, Ybus 86 nodes

For loadings we use two days of hourly data: an evening peak and per-customer noise, with a third of the customers exporting PV at midday. Day 1 trains the reductions; day 2 tests them, so every error below is measured on loadings the reduction has not seen. The ground truth is an AC power flow of the full model on day 2 (lowest phase-to-neutral voltage 220.8 V).

2. Neutral Kron reduction: is the neutral really at earth?

Eliminating the neutral conductor turns the four-wire model into a three-wire one. It is a Schur complement, Zabc = Zpp − Zpn Znn⁻¹ Z_np, and it is exact exactly when the neutral is at earth potential at both ends of every line. So the first step is not to reduce, but to check:

nk = kron_reduce_neutrals(net)
nk["_meta"]["kron_reduction"]["classification"]
# "grounded_neutral_projection", with 20 forced-ground buses

The reducer reports that it had to force the neutral to earth at 20 buses. Only the transformer bus was perfectly earthed; the MEN electrodes are 10 Ω, not 0 Ω, and the poles between them have no electrode at all. On the test day the cost is plain:

# neutral Kron: Ybus 86 → 66 nodes, worst voltage error 7.67 V

A 23% smaller matrix bought an error of almost 8 V, over 3% of the voltage, and it cannot be undone, because the neutral voltages it threw away are exactly the information that was needed. The scientific contract check_kron_boundary_recovery refuses the same transformation for the same reason.

Lesson 1

Neutral elimination is a statement about earthing, not about cables. Apply it only where the model earths the neutral perfectly (check for "classification" => "exact"); here we keep the four-wire model.

3. Simplification: exact, but what does it forget?

simplify_network merges lines through poles that carry nothing: no load, no shunt, one line in, one line out.

sp = simplify_network(net)
# simplify: 22 → 18 buses, worst error 5.68e-14 V, voltages kept for 17 of 21 LV buses

The error is round-off: merging series impedances through an empty bus is exact here because the cable has no shunt admittance. But four poles (p1, p2, p5, q1) no longer exist in the model, so their voltages are no longer computed. Pole p9 carries no customer either, yet it survives: its MEN electrode is a shunt that must stay attached somewhere.

Lesson 2

Simplification is exact for what it keeps and silent about what it removes. Use it when the voltages at pass-through poles do not matter. It cannot remove a pole that has a customer.

4. Opti-KRON: removing loaded buses, with a guarantee

To remove buses that do carry customers, the customers must move to a super-node. Opti-KRON chooses the fewest super-nodes that keep every voltage estimate within Ē, for every loading in an uncertainty set built from the training day:

m = opti_kron_model(net; H=4)
U = opti_kron_load_injections(m, net, train)       # 24 training loadings
res = opti_kron_reduction(m, OptiKronBox(U), 0.01; optimizer=HiGHS.Optimizer,
                          keep=opti_kron_required_buses(net))   # keep transformer buses

The choice of set is a choice of how much to trust the training day. Each set was reduced at two thresholds, then checked on the test day with the linear voltage model (the AC check follows in §5):

SetĒ = 0.005 p.u.test-day error (linear model)Ē = 0.010 p.u.test-day error (linear model)
scenarios (2 extreme hours)8 buses1.77 Ē5 buses1.38 Ē
convex hull of day 113 buses1.18 Ē6 buses0.91 Ē
box of day 113 buses0.50 Ē11 buses0.84 Ē

Read the table from top to bottom:

  • Scenarios are the classical approach: guarantee the bound at the heaviest and lightest hours. They keep the fewest buses but break the bound on an ordinary day, because the error depends on where the load is, not only on how much there is. PV export at a few poles is exactly such a pattern.
  • The convex hull guarantees every mixture of the training hours. That covers day 1 completely, but day 2 can still step outside it (1.18 Ē at the tighter threshold). A certificate covers its set, and only its set.
  • The box lets every customer vary independently within its observed range. It is wider, keeps more buses, and held on the test day.
Lesson 3

A smaller set means a smaller model and a weaker guarantee. Build the set from data that represent the conditions you will study, and keep a test day aside to find out whether it did.

5. Build, solve, lift, verify

kron_reduce_buses turns the box assignment at Ē = 0.01 into a solvable case:

red = kron_reduce_buses(net, m, res; Ebar=0.01)
# reduced case: 11 buses, 9 lines (1 equivalent), 3 shunts (1 equivalent), Ybus 42 nodes
# kept buses: lv0, mv, p1 … p8, r4

Opti-KRON kept the trunk up to p8 and the far end of lateral r. Lateral q folds into p4; the trunk end (p9 to p12) and the first three poles of lateral r fold into p8, which now reaches r4 through one equivalent line. Their customers sit on the kept poles, and the MEN electrodes at p9 and p12 reappear as one equivalent shunt at p8: earthing is not lost, it is re-expressed. Solve the reduced case for each test hour, and compare it with the full model:

pf  = solve_pf(with_loads(red, net, test, h); optimizer=ipopt)   # each test hour h
est = lift_solution(net, red, pf; model=m)
# Opti-KRON box Ē=0.01 (= 2.4 V): worst AC error on test day 2.14 V, after lift 0.25 V

The super-node voltages are within the 2.4 V bound in full AC power flow, not only in the linear model, and lift_solution brings the error down to 0.25 V. The reduced case has half the matrix size, and only 6 of the 15 customer poles remain as buses.

The workflow in four lines
  1. Check earthing before eliminating neutrals.
  2. Simplify when pass-through voltages don't matter.
  3. Reduce loaded buses with Opti-KRON, using a set built from representative data and transformer buses kept.
  4. Verify on loadings the reduction has not seen, and lift the answer back.
